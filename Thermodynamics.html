Introduction

By 2025, many Nobel Prizes in Physics and Chemistry have recognized work closely connected to thermodynamics, statistical mechanics, phase transitions, condensed matter physics, complex systems, and machine learning:

  • 1910 — Johannes Diderik van der Waals, Nobel Prize in Physics, for his work on the equation of state for gases and liquids.
  • 1918 — Max Planck, Nobel Prize in Physics, for his discovery of energy quanta.
  • 1926 — Jean Baptiste Perrin, Nobel Prize in Physics, for his work on the discontinuous structure of matter, especially his experimental demonstration of Brownian motion.
  • 1962 — Lev D. Landau, Nobel Prize in Physics, for his pioneering theories of condensed matter, especially liquid helium.
  • 1968 — Lars Onsager, Nobel Prize in Chemistry, for his discovery of the reciprocal relations bearing his name, which are fundamental to irreversible thermodynamics.
  • 1972 — John Bardeen, Leon N. Cooper, and John R. Schrieffer, Nobel Prize in Physics, for their jointly developed theory of superconductivity, usually called the BCS theory.
  • 1977 — Philip W. Anderson, Sir Nevill F. Mott, and John H. Van Vleck, Nobel Prize in Physics, for their fundamental theoretical investigations of the electronic structure of magnetic and disordered systems.
  • 1982 — Kenneth G. Wilson, Nobel Prize in Physics, for his theory of critical phenomena in connection with phase transitions.
  • 1991 — Pierre-Gilles de Gennes, Nobel Prize in Physics, for discovering that methods developed for studying order phenomena in simple systems can be generalized to more complex forms of matter, especially liquid crystals and polymers.
  • 1996 — David M. Lee, Douglas D. Osheroff, and Robert C. Richardson, Nobel Prize in Physics, for their discovery of superfluidity in helium-3.
  • 2001 — Eric A. Cornell, Wolfgang Ketterle, and Carl E. Wieman, Nobel Prize in Physics, for the achievement of Bose-Einstein condensation in dilute gases of alkali atoms, and for early fundamental studies of the properties of the condensates.
  • 2003 — Alexei A. Abrikosov, Vitaly L. Ginzburg, and Anthony J. Leggett, Nobel Prize in Physics, for pioneering contributions to the theory of superconductors and superfluids.
  • 2016 — David J. Thouless, F. Duncan M. Haldane, and J. Michael Kosterlitz, Nobel Prize in Physics, for theoretical discoveries of topological phase transitions and topological phases of matter.
  • 2021 — Giorgio Parisi, Nobel Prize in Physics, awarded jointly with Syukuro Manabe and Klaus Hasselmann. Parisi was recognized for the discovery of the interplay of disorder and fluctuations in physical systems from atomic to planetary scales.
  • 2024 — John J. Hopfield and Geoffrey E. Hinton, Nobel Prize in Physics, for foundational discoveries and inventions that enabled machine learning with artificial neural networks.
  • 2024 — David Baker, Demis Hassabis, and John M. Jumper, Nobel Prize in Chemistry, for computational protein design and protein-structure prediction, including advances associated with Rosetta and AlphaFold.

Machine learning and artificial intelligence have already demonstrated remarkable potential in scientific discovery. What comes next?
